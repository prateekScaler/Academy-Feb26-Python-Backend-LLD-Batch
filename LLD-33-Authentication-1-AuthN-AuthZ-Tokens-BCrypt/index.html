step 0 · a 5,000-year story

The fascinating history of authentication

“Trust, but verify.” Proving who you are is as old as civilization — so let’s tell it as one story: the same problem, solved again and again, each era handing its idea to the next.

🤔 Discuss first — Before computers, how did a Babylonian merchant, an Egyptian king, and a Roman soldier prove who they were? Across all of it there are just three tricks — can you name them?

▼
  • Something you know — a secret only you have: the Roman watchword, today a password or PIN.
  • Something you have — a possession hard to copy: a signet ring, a token, today your phone or a hardware key.
  • Something you are — a physical trait: a Babylonian fingerprint on clay, today Face ID / Touch ID.
  • Every modern login is still one (or more) of these three. Keep count as the story unfolds.
3500 BCE · Mesopotamia

A merchant needs a signature

Writing is brand new, but trade is already booming — and a deal pressed into a clay tablet is worthless unless you can prove who agreed to it. The Mesopotamian answer: a cylinder seal, a small carved stone rolled across the wet clay. The pattern it leaves is unique to its owner, hard to copy, and instantly checkable by anyone.

A few centuries later in Babylon, merchants go one better: they press a fingerprint into the clay. Now it isn’t an object vouching for you — it’s your own body.

👉 One chapter in, and two of the three factors are already on the table: something you have (the seal) and something you are (the fingerprint).

1000 BCE · Egypt & Rome

The king’s ring — and the first key revocation

Fast-forward two thousand years. Kingdoms now run on documents — decrees, treaties, tax orders — and a general has to know an order really came from the king. The answer travels on the king’s finger: a signet ring pressed into hot wax gives a unique pattern (identity), a seal that shatters if the letter is opened (tamper-evidence), and a mark the sender can’t later disown (non-repudiation).

That’s Tutankhamun’s actual ring in the photo. And here’s the detail engineers love: when a Pharaoh died, his ring was smashed the same day — so nobody could ever sign as a dead king. Egypt invented key revocation three thousand years before PKI.

⚡ Quick question — A wax seal promises identity, tamper-evidence and non-repudiation. What single modern mechanism makes exactly those three promises?

▼
  • A digital signature — it proves who signed (identity), breaks if the content is altered (integrity/tamper-evidence), and can’t be disowned (non-repudiation).
  • Hold the thought: in Step 5 you’ll meet the JWT signature, which makes the same three promises for API requests.
500–200 BCE · Rome

Friend or foe? Rome answers twice

Rome hits two identity problems a seal can’t solve. First: how do two allied families recognise each other generations later? They break a token — a tessera hospitalis — in two, one half per family. Decades on, total strangers prove the old alliance by fitting the halves together. (Greek symbolon, “thrown together” — the origin of our word symbol.)

Second: it’s midnight at the camp gate. You can’t see faces, and soldiers don’t wear rings. So each night a fresh watchword is inscribed on a wooden tablet and passed from the general down through the ranks. Speak it and you’re in; get it wrong and you’re treated as a spy.

👉 And there it is — the third factor, the one we still fight with daily: something you know. The world’s first password.

⚡ Quick question — Why did Rome change the watchword every single night?

▼
  • Because spoken secrets leak — every ear that hears one is a potential copy, and a password can be stolen without you ever knowing (unlike a ring).
  • Rotating it nightly limits the damage of any leak to a few hours — the exact reason your access tokens expire after 15–60 minutes in Step 5.

⏸️ Pause the story — notice the pattern

Three civilizations, three different problems — and every solution is one of just three tricks. In the 5,000 years since, nobody has found a fourth.

1940s · Bletchley Park

A war machine meets human laziness

Skip ahead to the biggest identity crisis ever staged: a world war fought over radio, where anyone can transmit anything. The German Enigma machine solved it with a shared secret — each day’s rotor order, ring settings and plugboard wiring. If a received message decrypted into readable German, it was authentic; gibberish meant wrong settings — or a fake.

Alan Turing’s team at Bletchley Park broke it anyway — and part of the reason was gloriously human: bored operators kept picking predictable message keys — girlfriends’ initials, repeated letters, the same patterns day after day.

👉 The strongest system in the world, undone by weak human choices. Sixty years later that’s still the #1 cause of breaches — remember it when we reach 123456.

1962 · MIT

The password meets the computer — and loses immediately

Then the computer arrives, and with it our problem in modern dress. MIT’s CTSS is one of the first time-sharing systems — many users, one machine — so for the first time software needs accounts, and accounts need passwords.

PhD student Allan Scherr is allotted four hours of compute a week. He wants more. His “hack”: request a printout of the password file. The file is stored in plaintext, so the operator hands him every password on the system, and he quietly shares them with friends.

The first computer password system in history was breached within months — not by breaking the door, but because the secrets were stored readable. (Scherr confessed 25 years later; MIT was amused.)

⚡ Quick question — What single change would have made Scherr’s printout worthless?

▼
  • Store something derived from the password instead of the password itself — a one-way transformation that lets you check a login attempt but never read the secret back.
  • That idea is the entire second half of today’s class — and it arrives in the very next chapter.
1976 · Bell Labs

Slow by design

The fix comes from the people building Unix. Robert Morris Sr. and Ken Thompson decide the password file should never contain passwords: instead, Unix crypt uses your password as a key to encrypt a block of zeros and stores the result — a transformation you can repeat to verify, but not reverse to read.

And one more stroke of genius: they repeat the transformation 25 times, tuned to take about one second on 1976 hardware — fast enough for a login, painfully slow for someone trying thousands of guesses.

👉 Both of today’s big ideas — store a hash (Step 3) and make it slow (Step 4) — were born right here, five decades ago.

step 1 · the two questions

Authentication ≠ Authorization

They’re said in one breath and mean different things. Keep them apart and half of auth gets simpler.

A club bouncer checks your ID at the door; then a wristband colour decides which rooms you can enter. Which step is authentication, which is authorization?

First who, then what.
  • The ID check answers “who are you?” — that’s authentication, and it comes first.
  • The wristband answers “what may you do?” (which rooms) — that’s authorization, decided after you’re identified.
  • Same passport, different boarding pass: your identity is not your permissions.
👤
AuthN — Authentication
“Who are you?” Verify identity — a password, a token, a fingerprint. Comes first. Fails with 401.
🎫
AuthZ — Authorization
“What may you do?” Given a known identity, is this action allowed? Comes after. Fails with 403.

✈️ The airport, and the codes

  • Check-in (AuthN): “passport, please” — verifies you are who you claim, issues a boarding pass.
  • Boarding gate (AuthZ): your ticket class decides economy / business / lounge — wrong flight? access denied.
  • 401 Unauthorized — “I don’t know who you are” (missing/invalid credentials) → show the login page. (Misnamed: it really means unauthenticated.)
  • 403 Forbidden — “I know who you are, but you can’t do this” → access-denied. Re-logging-in won’t help.

✅ The three A’s of security (AAA)

▼
  • Authentication — “who are you?” (verify identity).
  • Authorization — “what can you do?” (check permissions).
  • Accounting — “what did you do?” (the audit trail / logging — the Logging & Monitoring class later in this module).

🔍 Spot the mistake — three real snippets

Each snippet hides one classic AuthN/AuthZ blunder. Read the code, name the bug out loud — then click to check yourself.

# Snippet 1 — an EDITOR (logged in, valid session) calls this:
@app.delete("/events/<id>")
def delete_event(request, id):
    user = authenticate(request)              # succeeds — we know who this is
    if "event:delete" not in user.permissions:
        return Response(status=401)           # <- hmm.
    ...

🔍 Snippet 1 — what does the editor experience, and what should this return?

▼
  • 401 means “I don’t know who you are” — but authentication succeeded; the user merely lacks a permission. The right status is 403 Forbidden.
  • And it’s not cosmetic: most frontends react to a 401 by redirecting to the login page. The editor gets kicked out, logs back in, retries, gets kicked out again… an infinite login loop for what was a permission problem.
  • Rule of thumb: 401 → “show login”; 403 → “show access denied”.
# Snippet 2 — the decorators:
@require_permission("event:delete")    # outer — runs FIRST on each request
@require_login                         # inner — runs second
def delete_event(request, id):
    ...

🔍 Snippet 2 — an anonymous request arrives. What runs, in what order?

▼
  • Decorators wrap bottom-up, so the top one runs first on each request — here the permission check fires before anyone has authenticated.
  • So require_permission inspects user.permissions for an anonymous request: a crash at best — at worst a check that quietly passes and waves the request through.
  • Fix: swap the two lines — authenticate first, then authorize, so @require_login goes on top. The order of those two decorators is a real production bug, not a style choice.
// Snippet 3 — React: the delete button is only rendered for admins
{user.role === "admin" && <DeleteUserButton/>}

# ...and the Flask endpoint behind that button:
@app.delete("/users/<id>")
def delete_user(id):
    db.users.delete(id)     # no check — "only admins can see the button"

🔍 Snippet 3 — the button is hidden from non-admins. Are we safe?

▼
  • No. The browser is enemy territory: nothing stops curl -X DELETE https://api.example.com/users/7 — no button required.
  • Hiding UI is UX, not security. The backend must re-check the permission on every request, because the API can always be called directly.
  • Rule of thumb: the frontend decides what to show; only the backend decides what to allow.
🌐 Real world: Google (one login → Gmail vs Drive vs Admin Console) · GitHub (one account → read/write/admin per repo) · AWS (Access Key = AuthN, IAM policies = AuthZ). Same identity, different permissions.
step 2 · the foundation

Encoding vs Encryption vs Hashing

Before passwords, kill the confusion that sinks so many designs. Three words that all “transform data” — for three completely different jobs.

You must protect three things: (a) a user’s password, (b) an API key your app must later resend to Stripe, (c) a binary image you need to put inside a JSON body. One tool for all three?

Match the tool to whether you need the value back — and whether it’s a secret.
  • Password → hash (one-way): you only ever compare a login attempt; a breach yields nothing usable.
  • API key → encrypt (reversible with a key): you must recover the real key to send it to Stripe.
  • Image in JSON → encode (base64): not about secrecy at all — just making binary safe to carry as text.
  • Hashing the API key makes it unusable; encrypting the password adds a decryptable master key you don’t want to exist.

💡 The ELI5 — three everyday metaphors

Encoding — translating a letter (anyone with the dictionary can read it both ways)
"HELLO" → encode → "SEVMTE8=" → anyone decodes → "HELLO"
🔑 no key · rules are public · base64, URL-encoding, ASCII
Encryption — a locked diary (only the key-holder can read it)
"HELLO" + 🔑key → lock → "X#9@kL!mZ" → same 🔑key → "HELLO"
🔒 key required · reversible only with it · AES, RSA, Fernet
Hashing — a meat grinder (you cannot turn mince back into a steak)
"HELLO" → hash → "2cf24dba5fb0..." → ✗ cannot go back
🚫 no key, one-way · same input → same hash · SHA-256, bcrypt

📝 Letter examples you can do by hand

▼
  • Encoding (Pig Latin): HELLO → ELLOHAY. Anyone who knows the rule reverses it — no secret.
  • Encryption (Caesar shift 3): HELLO → KHOOR. To reverse you must know the key (shift 3); an attacker tries all 25 shifts.
  • Hashing (letter-sum): CAT → 3+1+20 = 24. But TAC and ATC also = 24 — many inputs collide to one output, so you can never reverse it. That’s one-way.

🧰 How base64 actually works — 3 bytes in, 4 characters out

Why does encoding exist at all? Channels like JSON, URLs and email headers can only carry text safely — raw bytes break them. base64 re-spells any bytes using 64 “safe” characters: A–Z a–z 0–9 + /. And 64 = 2⁶, so each character carries exactly 6 bits. That one fact explains everything else:

text          M          a          n
ASCII         77         97        110
bits       01001101   01100001   01101110      = 24 bits
regroup     010011  010110  000101  101110     = four 6-bit chunks
look up       19      22       5      46         (row in the base64 table)
result         T       W       F       u      ->  "Man"  =>  "TWFu"
  • 3 bytes → 4 characters, always — that’s the ~33% size growth of anything base64’d.
  • Input not a multiple of 3? Pad the output with =: "Ma" → "TWE=", "M" → "TQ==". That’s all the mysterious trailing equals signs ever meant.
  • Decoding is the same table read backwards. Notice what’s missing: a key. Nothing secret happens anywhere — which is exactly why base64 is not security.
  • JWTs (Step 5) use base64url — the same scheme with - and _ instead of + and /, so tokens survive being pasted into URLs.

🧮 When do I use which? — a decision guide

▼
  • “Store a password” → hash (bcrypt / argon2).
  • “Send a secret only the recipient can read” → encrypt (AES / TLS).
  • “Store an API key / card token I must use later” → encrypt (reversible).
  • “Put binary (an image) inside JSON” → encode (base64).
  • “Verify a file wasn’t tampered with” → hash (SHA-256 checksum).
⚠️ base64 is a costume, not a lock. c2VjcmV0MTIz looks scrambled but anyone decodes it instantly. The three worst mistakes: encoding for security, encrypting passwords (a stolen key reverses everyone’s), and hashing a secret you need to read back (you can never recover it).
step 3 · the main event

Password storage — a history of getting breached

When an attacker dumps your users table, the damage depends entirely on how you stored passwords. Each stage was “secure” until an attack broke it.

🤔 Discuss first — An attacker just downloaded your entire users table to the dark web. Company-ending catastrophe, or a shrug? What does it come down to?

▼
  • It depends entirely on how you stored the passwords — the same breach is a disaster or a non-event.
  • Plaintext → instant, total takeover of every account (and every other site where users reused the password).
  • MD5 / SHA → cracked in minutes-to-hours with precomputed tables and GPUs.
  • bcrypt / argon2 → the attacker holds hashes that cost years each to crack — effectively worthless.
  • So “don’t get breached” isn’t the only goal — “a breach reveals nothing useful” is the real one.

🔌 The four eras — and the attack that ended each

1 · Plaintext (1960s–80s)

What: the password stored exactly as typed; login is a string compare.

Broken by: everything. A breach (or a curious DBA, or a backup tape) is instant, total account takeover.

db.insert(username, password)      # stored as-is 😱
☠️ Facebook, 2019: passwords logged in plaintext since 2012 — 600 million users, readable by 20,000+ employees.
2 · Fast hash — MD5 / SHA-1 (1990s)

What: a one-way hash: “can’t reverse it, so the DB is safe.”

Broken by: rainbow tables + speed. Precompute hashes for billions of common passwords once, then every leaked hash is an instant lookup. And no salt means two users with the same password share a hash — the leak reveals it.

md5("password") == md5("password")   # same for everyone → look it up
☠️ LinkedIn, 2012: 117 million unsalted SHA-1 hashes leaked — 90% cracked within days. Top password found: 123456 (753,305 users).
3 · Salted hash (2000s)

What: a unique random salt per user, hashed with the password — kills rainbow tables.

Broken by: GPU brute force. Salt defeats tables (a precomputed table can’t cover 2¹²⁸ salts), but SHA-256 still runs at ~22 billion/sec on one GPU, so a weak 8-char password falls in minutes. Salting stops tables, not trying.

sha256(salt + password)   # tables defeated... but still far too fast

🧮 Where does “falls in minutes” come from? The simple math

▼

Think of a password as a combination lock: a 3-digit padlock has 10 × 10 × 10 = 1,000 combinations. A “weak 8-character password” (lowercase + digits) is just a lock with 8 dials and 36 symbols per dial:

symbols per slot    a-z (26) + 0-9 (10)          =  36 choices
slots               8 characters
total passwords     36 x 36 x ... x 36  =  36^8    ~  2,800,000,000,000   (2.8 trillion)

one GPU (SHA-256)   22,000,000,000 guesses/sec        (22 billion)
worst case          2.8 trillion / 22 billion     ~  128 sec  ~  2 minutes
on average          found halfway through          ~  1 minute
  • Where’s the salt in this math? Nowhere — and that surprises people. The salt is stored in plaintext right next to the hash, so the attacker already has it; they just compute sha256(salt + guess). Salt never makes one user harder to crack — it forces the attacker to redo this whole 2-minute job per user instead of cracking everyone at once with one precomputed table.
  • The exponent is the whole game. Add one character: ×36 → ~1.2 hours. Lowercase only (26⁸)? 9.5 seconds. But 12 lowercase letters (26¹²) → ~50 days, and 12 characters from the full keyboard (95¹²) → ~780,000 years. Length beats cleverness, every time.
  • Now swap the denominator — that’s the next section. Same 2.8 trillion guesses, but bcrypt allows ~1,400/sec instead of 22 billion: 2.8 trillion ÷ 1,400 ≈ 63 years. One division explains the entire “slow by design” strategy.
4 · Slow + salted — bcrypt / scrypt / argon2 (2010s+)

What: a deliberately slow hash with a tunable work factor, salt built in.

Broken by: — nothing practical, and that’s the point. This is the answer we ship. (argon2 is memory-hard too, to blunt GPUs/ASICs.)

bcrypt.hashpw(password, bcrypt.gensalt(rounds=12))   # ~0.3s per hash, salt inside

🤔 Hold on — are bcrypt / scrypt / argon2 encryption or hashing? What are they, technically?

▼
  • Hashing, one hundred percent. One-way, no key, nothing is ever decrypted: at login we re-run the function on your attempt and compare outputs. The original password is never recovered from the database — not even by us.
  • Technically they’re password-hashing functions (the textbook term is key-derivation functions, KDFs). Ordinary hashes like SHA-256 are built for speed; these three are engineered for cost, each with its own trick: bcrypt — tunable CPU rounds (each +1 doubles the work) · scrypt — also demands a pile of RAM, so a GPU with 10,000 tiny cores starves for memory · argon2 — 2015 Password Hashing Competition winner, lets you dial CPU, RAM and threads independently; the modern default.
  • The confusion is understandable — and here’s the fun twist: bcrypt is built out of an encryption cipher (Blowfish — that’s the “b”). It uses your password as the key to encrypt a fixed string, 64 times over, then throws the key away and stores the result. Encryption machinery, run in a way nobody can reverse. Sound familiar? It’s exactly Unix crypt’s 1976 trick from the History section — DES then, Blowfish now.
  • Litmus test for the whole class: could you ever need the original value back? Encryption answers yes (that’s why it has a key). These answer never — and that’s what makes them hashing.

Two users pick the password letmein. With a per-user salt, the stored hashes are…

Salt = different hashes for the same password.
  • A unique random salt per user means letmein hashes to two different values — so one precomputed rainbow table can’t crack both, and a breach doesn’t reveal that they share a password.
  • But salting does not slow anything down — SHA-256 is still billions/sec. That’s a separate problem, solved by a slow hash (next).
  • The “also makes each guess slow” option conflates the two: salt kills tables; a high work factor kills brute force.

🧠 Hashing 101 — rapid-fire doubts

Five questions that come up in every batch. Commit to an answer out loud — then click.

🕵️ The conspiracy theory: “whoever invented SHA-256 must secretly keep the un-hash function. ‘Irreversible’ is a lie.” True or false?

Irreversible isn’t a promise — it’s arithmetic.
  • A hash doesn’t scramble your data — that’s encryption, and encryption does have an unscramble (the key). A hash throws information away: hash one letter or a 4 GB movie, out come exactly 256 bits either way. A movie can’t hide inside 32 bytes — what you’d need to go back simply isn’t there.
  • Feel it with a mini-hash — “add the digits”: 7+8 = 15. Now reverse “15”. Was it 7+8? 9+6? 14+1? Easy forward, information-losing backward — and no secret decoder exists for anyone, because the answer isn’t hidden, it’s gone.
  • “Then how does login work?!” — we never un-hash. We hash your attempt and compare the two outputs. One-way is all a login ever needs.

Two different inputs can produce the same SHA-256 hash (a collision). A student says that alone proves hashing is broken. Who’s right?

Existing in theory ≠ findable in practice.
  • Pigeonhole logic: infinitely many possible inputs, “only” 2²⁵⁶ outputs — so collisions are guaranteed to exist. Hashing was never sold as collision-free, only collision-unfindable.
  • How unfindable? 2²⁵⁶ is on the order of the number of atoms in the observable universe. Every computer on Earth, hashing non-stop since the Big Bang, would still not be expected to hit one SHA-256 collision.
  • “Broken” means craftable on demand: MD5 collisions since 2004 (seconds on a laptop), SHA-1 since 2017 (Google’s “SHAttered”). That’s exactly why era 2 above is dead while SHA-256 is still fine.

sha256("kitten") gives the same output every time, on every machine, forever. True or false — and is that a strength or a bug?

Deterministic AND one-way — no contradiction.
  • Same input → same hash is the whole point: login compares hash(attempt) to the stored value; a checksum re-hashes your download. Randomize the output and neither could ever match.
  • The doubt underneath: “if it always maps the same way, surely you can map back?” The meat grinder answers it: the same steak always gives the same mince — and you still can’t un-grind it. Repeatable and irreversible coexist just fine.
  • So where did “two users, different hashes” come from a moment ago? Not from the function — from the salt we deliberately mix in. SHA-256 itself never improvises; we add the randomness, and store it openly.

You hash a 5-character password and a 4 GB movie, both with SHA-256. The two outputs are…

Fixed-size output — and it quietly proves irreversibility.
  • SHA-256 always emits exactly 256 bits — that’s the “-256” in the name. "hi" and a Blu-ray rip get the same 64 hex characters.
  • This is the cleanest proof of one-way-ness you’ll ever get: if 4 GB went in and 32 bytes came out, virtually everything about the input was discarded. You cannot reconstruct what was never stored.
  • Practical bonus: whatever users pick as passwords, your DB column is one fixed width, and comparing two hashes costs the same whether the input was a tweet or a terabyte.

You capitalise one letter: kitten → kitteN. The hash changes only slightly… right?

The avalanche effect — no “getting warmer”.
  • Real values: sha256("kitten") = 58972659401cbee9… but sha256("kitteN") = ea64c998d3b05f0d… — one flipped bit of input, two unrelated outputs.
  • It’s deliberate. If similar inputs gave similar hashes, an attacker could play “getting warmer”: tweak a guess, watch the hash drift toward the target, and hill-climb straight to your password.
  • Avalanche destroys that gradient — every guess is all-or-nothing, no feedback, no shortcuts. The only attack left is trying passwords one at a time… which is precisely the fight bcrypt’s slowness (next section) is built to win.
step 4 · the gold standard

Why “slow” is the whole trick

It feels backwards to want a slow function — but this is exactly the “slow by design” idea born in Unix crypt (1976). Here’s the asymmetry that makes it brilliant.

⚖️ The core insight: a legitimate user logs in once and never notices a 0.3-second hash. An attacker trying billions of guesses cannot afford 0.3 seconds each. Same cost, opposite impact.

🔧 What bcrypt gives you

  • Salt, built in. gensalt() generates and stores a random salt inside the hash string — you can’t forget to salt.
  • A work factor (cost / rounds). rounds=12 is standard; each +1 doubles the time. As hardware gets faster, you raise the number — the same algorithm stays ahead. That’s adaptive security.
  • Designed to resist parallelism, so a GPU’s thousands of cores help far less than they do against SHA.

✅ The rules, in one breath: use bcrypt (cost ≥ 12) or argon2; use the library, never roll your own; never MD5/SHA/unsalted for passwords; and compare with a constant-time check (hmac.compare_digest) so verification can’t leak via timing.

⏱️ Wait — what’s a “constant-time check”, and what problem does it solve?

▼

The problem: Python’s == is lazy in exactly the wrong way. Comparing two strings, it walks left to right and quits at the first mismatch:

stored:  "a3f9c2e8..."
guess:   "b1111111..."   -> first char wrong -> == returns in ~1 step   (FAST)
guess:   "a3f91111..."   -> 4 chars match    -> == walks 5 steps        (slower)
guess:   "a3f9c2e1..."   -> 7 chars match    -> == walks 8 steps        (slower still)
  • See the leak? The response time whispers how much of the guess was right. An attacker doesn’t need to see the stored value — they watch the clock. It’s the movie safecracker with an ear on the dial, listening for the click of each correct digit.
  • That turns an impossible search into a trivial one: instead of guessing all 16⁶⁴ possibilities of a 64-hex secret, lock in character 1 (the guess that ran slightly slower), then character 2… — 16 × 64 tries instead of 16⁶⁴. With enough repeated requests to average out network noise, this works against real APIs verifying tokens and webhook signatures.
  • The fix: hmac.compare_digest(a, b) compares every byte, always, no early exit — a wrong first character and a wrong last character take exactly the same time. Nothing to hear, no clicks, no gradient: the timing side-channel goes silent.
  • Where you’ll actually use it: any hand-rolled comparison of secrets — verifying a JWT’s signature (code/04), a webhook’s HMAC, an API token against its stored hash. code/03_verifying_safely_timing.py demonstrates the leak and the fix in runnable tests. (bcrypt’s own checkpw does this internally — one more reason to use the library.)

Why does making the password hash slower improve security, when it’s the same function for user and attacker?

It’s an asymmetry, not a speed bump.
  • One legitimate hash per login is imperceptible; the same per-guess cost, multiplied by billions of guesses, turns hours into centuries.
  • Output length isn’t the point — hashes are already irreversible; the defense is the cost per attempt.
  • Salt and work factor solve different problems: salt kills rainbow tables, the work factor kills brute force. You need both.
step 5 · staying logged in

Tokens — proving identity after login

You proved who you are once. How does every next request know it’s still you, without re-entering the password?

🎭 Two approaches

🗄️
Session (stateful)
Server stores a session record (DB/Redis) and hands the client a session-id cookie. Every request looks that id up. Revoke = delete the row.
🎫
Token / JWT (stateless)
Server hands the client a signed token holding the claims. No server storage — it just verifies the signature. “Like a movie ticket: it proves you paid without the theatre phoning the box office.”

🧩 A JWT is three base64url parts

header . payload . signature

header    {"alg":"HS256","typ":"JWT"}                 # metadata
payload   {"sub":"42","role":"admin","exp":1780...}  # your claims (the data)
signature HMAC_SHA256(header + "." + payload, SECRET) # the tamper-proof seal
💬 What it does: The server signs header.payload with a secret only it knows. Anyone can read the parts (base64), but only the server can produce a valid signature — so a forged or edited token fails verification.
⚠️ Signed, NOT encrypted. The payload is only base64-encoded — anyone holding the token can decode and read every claim. Never put a secret (card number, password, PII) in a JWT. The signature protects integrity, not confidentiality.

🔄 Access + refresh tokens — the usual answer to revocation

▼
  • Access token: short-lived (15–60 min), sent on every API call. If stolen, it expires fast.
  • Refresh token: long-lived (days), sent only to a /refresh endpoint to mint a new access token — so the user stays logged in without re-entering the password.
  • This bounds the damage of the “JWTs are hard to revoke” problem: a leaked access token dies in minutes; you revoke by refusing to refresh (and can keep a small blacklist / token-version for the rest).

A teammate wants to store the user’s credit-card number in the JWT payload “so we don’t re-fetch it.” The problem?

JWT payload is public — signed, not encrypted.
  • Anyone holding the token can base64-decode the payload and read every claim — no secret required. A card number there is effectively plaintext.
  • The signature only proves the payload wasn’t tampered with; it does not hide it. Confidentiality would need actual encryption.
  • Size is a real (secondary) downside, but the security bug is the leak — keep secrets out of the payload; store a reference (an id) and fetch server-side.
step 6 · the authz model

RBAC — from identity to permissions

Now that you know who they are, decide what they may do — without scattering role checks across the codebase.

ROLE_PERMISSIONS = {
    "viewer": {"event:read"},
    "editor": {"event:read", "event:write"},
    "admin":  {"event:read", "event:write", "event:delete", "user:manage"},
}

def can(user, permission):
    return permission in union(ROLE_PERMISSIONS[r] for r in user.roles)

# at the point of action, check the PERMISSION, never the role name:
if not user.can("event:delete"):
    raise PermissionError
💬 What it does: Users have roles; roles grant permissions; you check the fine-grained permission at each action. Add a “moderator” role tomorrow and nothing else changes — whereas scattered if role == "admin" checks would each need editing.
🔗 You’ve seen this shape before: it’s the LLD-29 Membership association class — the (User × Group) pairing carried a role. RBAC is that idea, generalised into roles → permissions. (More flexible still is ABAC — decide from many attributes: owner, department, time-of-day.)

Why check user.can("event:delete") at each action instead of user.role == "admin"?

Check the permission, not the role name.
  • A permission check asks the real question (“may this action happen?”); the role→permission mapping lives in one place, so new roles or re-assigned permissions don’t touch the call sites.
  • if role == "admin" hard-codes policy into every check — add a “moderator” who can delete and you’re hunting through the codebase (and will miss one).
  • It’s not about speed or secrecy — it’s about keeping authorization policy in a single, changeable place.
